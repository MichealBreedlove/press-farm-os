The Half-Year at a Glance

Press Farm is a half-acre specialty garden in Yountville supplying Chef Philip Tessier's kitchens. This report covers the last six months (December 2025 – May 2026), sets them against the farm's full history, looks ahead to the rest of 2026 and the years beyond, and answers the question on the table for the fall planting meeting: the farm has outgrown its half-acre, and the next step is more land.

1

The half-year delivered $48.7K across 91 deliveries — a healthy winter-and-spring run, carried by a standout January ($19.8K) on the back of a big carrot harvest. The farm enters its strong season (August–November) with momentum and a waiting list of demand it cannot yet fill.

2

Every square foot is already proven gold. A half-acre is producing a trailing-twelve-month run rate near $124K — roughly $200K+ per acre, several times typical specialty-crop benchmarks. The model is validated; the obvious move is to run it across more ground.

3

The farm is now supply-constrained, not demand-constrained. The best crops sell out, the August–September peak leaves orders unfilled, and winter carrots clear everything that's picked. With the second hire lifting the labor ceiling, land is the one lever left to pull.

The Bottom Line for the Planting Meeting

Press Farm has done everything a half-acre can do. The economics are proven at ~$200K+ per acre, the kitchen wants more than the farm can grow at peak, and storable winter crops like carrots sell out every January. The growth lever is no longer intensity — it's acreage. Secure additional ground now (the neighboring wineries make this realistic and cheap to lease), staff it with the incoming hire, and plant it to the crops already proven to sell. More land is the highest-return investment in front of the farm.

In-season margin on what the farm grows remains above 90%. The economics are not the problem; capacity is.

A note on labor in the numbers

Labor and grower pay are not yet captured in the system, so these figures are contribution, not fully-loaded net. The fix is cheap — log hours for one month — and it matters before any decision to hire or expand, because labor is precisely the lever this report turns on.

02Two Houses, One Garden

Press Farm has supplied both Press and Under-Study since Under-Study opened. Because deliveries to the two houses have always been booked together under one account, the system cannot yet split how much produce flows to each kitchen — it has been kept, correctly, as one combined relationship.

Correcting an earlier read

An earlier draft of this analysis treated the farm as a "single-account" operation with concentration risk. That was a bookkeeping artifact, not reality. The farm has served two restaurants all along — the revenue simply wasn't tagged by house. The relationship is a two-venue partnership, and it is a strength, not a fragility.

The one practical gap this creates is planning: without a per-house split, the farm can't yet see whether Press and Under-Study want different things, or how fast each is growing. That matters directly for fall planting — if the two kitchens pull different crops or volumes, the garden should be planted to serve both menus deliberately.

Recommendation
Start tagging deliveries by house this fall

Going forward, mark each delivery as Press or Under-Study (the system already supports both as separate restaurants). Within one season this produces a clean per-house demand picture — what each kitchen orders, when, and how much — so the crop plan can be built around two menus instead of an estimate. No back-fixing of history is needed; the split simply starts now.

Citrus and yuzu from the orchard round out winter. Plant what already sells before planting anything speculative.

05The Case for More Land

Should Press Farm grow beyond its half-acre? The data gives a clear answer: yes — acquiring more land is the single highest-return move the farm can make. The model is proven, demand outruns supply, and the labor ceiling is lifting. The only thing missing is ground.

5.1  The farm today is fully built out

The half-acre is laid out in defined blocks — the corn field (8 rows, corn and fava), the tomato field (22 rows), the field beside the corn (14 rows), the orchard (17 rows), the passionfruit field (15 rows), plus permanent flower rows along the back and sides. Roughly 75+ production rows at 25′ × 30″, worked at full intensity. There is no slack left to plant into — every block is in rotation, which is exactly why the next unit of growth has to come from new ground.

The number that makes the case

A half-acre is producing a ~$124K trailing-twelve-month run rate — about $200,000+ per acre. Typical California specialty-vegetable ground grosses a fraction of that; even intensive market gardens reach only $50–100K per acre. Press Farm runs at two to four times that because it grows high-value specialty crops for a fine-dining kitchen. When each acre is worth $200K+, the most valuable thing the farm can buy is another acre.

5.4  Labor scales with the land

One real input has to grow alongside the ground: hands. The good news is that the labor expansion is already underway — a second hire is in process, and the work argues for completing it. Each new block of proven, high-margin crops more than pays for the labor to tend it, so labor is not a barrier to expansion but a companion investment that the new revenue funds. Pair every new block of land with the crew to work it, and the farm grows in balance.

The verdict

Acquire more land — start now. Lease winery-adjacent ground, phase it in block by block as the second hire (and seasonal help) come online, and plant it to the crops already proven to sell: winter carrots and roots, the supply-short peak-season flowers and herbs, and selective perennials. At ~$200K+ per acre with demand outrunning supply, every acre the farm can staff is a six-figure opportunity. The half-acre proved the model — more land is how it scales.
