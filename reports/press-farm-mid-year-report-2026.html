The Half-Year at a Glance

Press Farm is a half-acre specialty garden in Yountville supplying Chef Philip Tessier's kitchens. This report covers the last six months (December 2025 – May 2026), sets them against the farm's full history, looks ahead to the rest of 2026 and the years beyond, and answers the question on the table for the fall planting meeting: does adding acreage help the farm, or not?

1

The half-year delivered $48.7K across 91 deliveries — a healthy winter-and-spring run, carried by a standout January ($19.8K) on the back of a big carrot harvest. The farm enters its strong season (August–November) with momentum.

2

The land is already working at an elite intensity. A half-acre is producing a trailing-twelve-month run rate near $124K — roughly $200K+ per acre, several times typical specialty-crop benchmarks. The farm is not under-using its ground.

3

The binding constraint is labor and harvest capacity, not land. One grower plus a weekly helper is near the management ceiling of the current half-acre. That single fact is the key to the acreage decision below.

The Bottom Line for the Planting Meeting

Adding raw acreage "on spec" would mostly add weeding and waste, because the farm sells nearly everything it can pick and the limit is hands, not dirt. But targeted expansion — a dedicated winter root/brassica block to deepen the proven January pull, planted into leased winery-adjacent ground and staffed by the incoming second hire — pays for itself in under a season. Expand by crop and by demand, not by the acre.

1.2  What it cost

Cash inputs for the six months were $10,243 — but $5,074 of that was equipment (one-time buildout), leaving roughly $3,600 in recurring biological inputs (seed, soil, plants, amendments) against $48.7K of revenue. In-season margin on what the farm grows remains above 90%. The economics are not the problem; capacity is.

A note on labor in the numbers

Labor and grower pay are not yet captured in the system, so these figures are contribution, not fully-loaded net. The fix is cheap — log hours for one month — and it matters before any decision to hire or expand, because labor is precisely the lever this report turns on.

02Two Houses, One Garden

Press Farm has supplied both Press and Under-Study since Under-Study opened. Because deliveries to the two houses have always been booked together under one account, the system cannot yet split how much produce flows to each kitchen — it has been kept, correctly, as one combined relationship.

Correcting an earlier read

An earlier draft of this analysis treated the farm as a "single-account" operation with concentration risk. That was a bookkeeping artifact, not reality. The farm has served two restaurants all along — the revenue simply wasn't tagged by house. The relationship is a two-venue partnership, and it is a strength, not a fragility.

The one practical gap this creates is planning: without a per-house split, the farm can't yet see whether Press and Under-Study want different things, or how fast each is growing. That matters directly for fall planting — if the two kitchens pull different crops or volumes, the garden should be planted to serve both menus deliberately.

Recommendation
Start tagging deliveries by house this fall

Going forward, mark each delivery as Press or Under-Study (the system already supports both as separate restaurants). Within one season this produces a clean per-house demand picture — what each kitchen orders, when, and how much — so the crop plan can be built around two menus instead of an estimate. No back-fixing of history is needed; the split simply starts now.

Citrus and yuzu from the orchard round out winter. Plant what already sells before planting anything speculative.

05The Acreage Question

Should Press Farm grow beyond its half-acre? The honest answer depends entirely on how — and the data points to a clear yes-with-conditions.

5.1  The farm today

The half-acre is laid out in defined blocks — the corn field (8 rows, corn and fava), the tomato field (22 rows), the field beside the corn (14 rows), the orchard (17 rows), the passionfruit field (15 rows), plus permanent flower rows along the back and sides. Roughly 75+ production rows at 25′ × 30″, worked by one grower with a weekly helper for weeding.

The number that frames everything

A half-acre is producing a ~$124K trailing-twelve-month run rate — about $200,000+ per acre. Typical California specialty-vegetable ground grosses a fraction of that. Intensive market gardens reach $50–100K per acre. Press Farm is running at two to four times that, because it grows high-value garnish and specialty crops for a fine-dining kitchen. The land is not the underused asset.

5.4  The real gate: labor, then land

Two facts from the ground decide this. First, land may be available through neighboring wineries — so ground is not the scarce input. Second, the farm is run by one grower with a Friday helper, and a second hire is already in process. That hire — not the dirt — is the true unlock. With a second pair of hands, a targeted winter block and a fully-harvested peak both become reachable. Without it, more acreage would stretch one person past the breaking point and revenue would leak as unharvested crop.

The acreage verdict

Lease, don't buy; expand by the block, not the acre; and let the second hire — not the land — set the pace. A dedicated winter carrot/brassica block is the highest-confidence first move: it deepens the proven January peak, stores well, and fills the farm's softest season. Hold off on generic acreage until labor is in place and a per-house demand picture exists.
